The small-business website launch checklist.

From business facts to the first test enquiry: check what a customer will actually use before calling a website finished.

A practical review before you share the link.

This checklist is for a real small-business website, not just an attractive preview. It reflects the checks JowkariTech uses when preparing a draft and discussing its production launch. Work through the sections and note what needs a real provider or a business-owner decision.

By Mohammad, JowkariTech · Updated 2026-10-12

1. Business details: make the facts agree.

Write one approved version of the business name, phone number, email, services and service area. Use those facts on the website and your Google Business Profile. A public profile does not make an incorrect phone number or old opening time useful.

Only show a customer-facing street address when it is genuinely appropriate for visits. Do not create fictional branches to cover nearby cities. For a service-area business, describe where the team actually works. Confirm who can answer enquiries and what happens outside normal availability.

2. Navigation: put the customer’s question first.

Choose the essential pages before adding every possible feature: the main offer, the actual services, a credible business introduction and a clear way to get in touch. Use descriptive link text such as “roof repair services” rather than several identical “learn more” buttons.

For a roofer, a planned replacement and a leak enquiry may need different information. For a plumber, routine fixture work should not be confused with emergency availability. A small business does not need a separate near-identical page for every neighbourhood.

3. Content and images: replace the placeholders.

Read every headline and service description aloud. Remove wording that could fit any business and add the facts that help a visitor decide: what is included, who the service suits, the real service area and the next step.

Use images you own or have permission to publish. Label a concept as a concept. Caption real project photos accurately, and get permission before displaying customer feedback. Do not let AI fill gaps with invented awards, licences, response times or years in business.

4. Mobile layout: test the page, not just the screenshot.

Open the draft in desktop, tablet and phone previews. Look for text that spills sideways, buttons that overlap, menus that hide useful pages and images that crop out important details. Increase the browser text size and confirm that the content remains usable.

Try keyboard navigation as well as clicks. You should be able to reach links and form fields, see which control has focus and read form labels without relying on placeholder text. Check that image descriptions describe useful information, not a list of keywords.

5. Enquiries: prove where a message goes.

Click the real phone, email and quote actions. Submit one clearly marked test through the production enquiry form and confirm it reaches the intended inbox. A success message from the browser is not the same as verified inbox receipt.

Check the failure path too: wrong email format, missing required information and a lost internet connection. A failed submission should explain what happened and keep the visitor’s input. Do not publish a booking button until it connects to a real scheduling process.

6. Studio export: know what is included.

In JowkariTech Studio, create a draft, select a section to upload a photo, and use Preview mode to test links. The Details panel offers optional AI text refinement. Add extra pages from Pages; the starting draft does not automatically create a full multi-page company website.

Save a project JSON backup before moving devices. The downloaded ZIP contains a static website and its editable project file. Its enquiry form prepares an email in the visitor’s email app; it does not automatically send through a hosted lead inbox. Connect and test a form service for that behaviour.

7. Domain and cost: keep ownership clear.

Keep the domain in an account controlled by the business. Record who manages DNS, hosting, backups and renewal notices. Use delegated access where supported instead of sharing passwords in email or chat.

Distinguish one-time build costs from recurring domain, hosting, email and paid integration costs. JowkariTech’s C$150 focused setup is not a promise of free lifetime domain renewal or unlimited custom development. Confirm your scope before approving the work.

8. Search readiness: make the useful pages reachable.

Give each important page a clear title, one main heading and a descriptive summary. Link to it from another useful page on the site. Keep the preferred URL consistent in internal links, canonical markup and the XML sitemap.

Keep drafts, expired previews and test pages out of the indexable sitemap. Make sure intended landing pages are available without login and are not marked noindex. Search Console URL Inspection reports indexing status; submitting a sitemap does not force Google to index or rank a page.

9. Business Profile: connect the visitor’s next step.

When your Google Business Profile is public, check its website destination while signed out. It should open the correct page, not a login, a temporary preview or a broken redirect. Compare the visible business information with the website.

Add only genuine business photos and ask actual customers for honest feedback without incentives or review gating. Relevance, distance and prominence affect local results; website edits alone do not guarantee a top-three placement.

10. After launch: keep a small maintenance routine.

Keep the approved project backup, record the published URLs and check the site again on a real phone. Re-test enquiries after changing providers, DNS or form settings. Remove expired offers and correct business details when they change.

Use Search Console to separate indexed pages from pages still unknown or awaiting indexing. Track meaningful enquiries alongside search visits. A small number of impressions is not enough evidence that a website ranks broadly for a competitive search term.

Three useful references.

Google explains how sitemaps support discovery, the minimum technical requirements for Search, and how local Business Profile rankings work. Those documents do not promise ranking or indexing after a website change.
